Looking for **cybersecurity jobs** can feel like searching one job market when you are really looking at several different careers. A SOC analyst investigates alerts; a GRC analyst works with risk and compliance; an application security engineer helps developers ship safer software; and a penetration tester assesses systems with permission. The titles overlap, but the day-to-day work does not.

This guide maps the main cybersecurity career paths, explains what employers tend to ask for, and gives you a practical plan to choose a role and apply. It focuses on the U.S. market for salary and labor data; job titles, requirements, and compensation vary by country and employer. If your main question is how to break into the field from scratch, pair this overview with our [roadmap for breaking into cybersecurity](/career/breaking-into-cybersecurity-2026/).

## Cybersecurity jobs at a glance

| Job family | Common titles | Typical work | A good fit if you enjoy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Security operations | SOC analyst, security analyst, detection analyst | Reviewing alerts, investigating suspicious activity, escalating incidents | Pattern-finding, procedures, teamwork |
| Incident response | Incident responder, DFIR analyst, threat hunter | Scoping incidents, collecting evidence, containing threats | Investigation and calm decision-making |
| Governance, risk, and compliance | GRC analyst, security compliance analyst, IT auditor | Assessing risk, mapping controls, gathering evidence, writing policies | Writing, organizing, stakeholder conversations |
| Identity and access management | IAM analyst, identity engineer, access governance analyst | Managing accounts, authentication, permissions, access reviews | Systems thinking and careful process |
| Vulnerability management | Vulnerability analyst, vulnerability management engineer | Prioritizing weaknesses, coordinating fixes, tracking remediation | Turning technical findings into practical action |
| Offensive security | Penetration tester, security consultant, red team operator | Testing authorized systems, validating vulnerabilities, writing reports | Technical problem-solving and explaining risk |
| Application security | Application security engineer, product security analyst | Reviewing code and designs, testing applications, helping developers fix issues | Programming and collaboration |
| Cloud security | Cloud security analyst, cloud security engineer | Securing cloud identities, configurations, workloads, and data | Infrastructure, automation, continuous learning |

These are broad job families, not a complete list. The [NIST NICE Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/applied-cybersecurity/nice/nice-framework-resource-center) gives employers and job seekers a shared vocabulary for cybersecurity work, skills, and tasks. A company may combine several work roles under one title, so compare responsibilities in a posting instead of relying on its title alone.

## What cybersecurity jobs are in demand?

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) tracks **information security analysts**, one established occupation within the wider cybersecurity market. It reports a median annual wage of **$124,910 in May 2024** and projects **29% employment growth from 2024 to 2034**, with about **16,000 openings per year on average**. Those numbers describe this BLS occupation in the United States; they are not a salary promise or a measure of every cybersecurity job. The BLS also notes that many analysts enter with related IT experience, although education and experience requirements differ by employer. See the [BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/computer-and-information-technology/information-security-analysts.htm) for underlying wage, outlook, and state-level data.

Growth projections do not mean every applicant will find an entry-level opening quickly. Hiring conditions vary by region, seniority, industry, and the skills an employer needs. ISC2's [2025 hiring-trends research](https://www.isc2.org/Insights/2025/09/cybersecurity-hiring-trends-skills-deep-dive) surveyed hiring managers in six countries. It found that teamwork, problem-solving, and analytical thinking ranked highly for early-career candidates, alongside technical knowledge. Employers need people who can explain what they found, document it, and work with the teams responsible for fixing it.

For a realistic local picture, compare recent postings in your target location, check requirements on employers' own career sites, and consult official wage data for your state or metro area. Treat job-board salary estimates as rough comparisons: they may mix seniority levels, bonuses, and unrelated titles.

## 8 common cybersecurity career paths

### 1. SOC analyst and security analyst

A SOC (security operations center) analyst monitors alerts and investigates activity that may indicate an attack. Tasks can include checking endpoint and identity logs, validating alerts in a SIEM, documenting findings, and escalating incidents using a playbook. Some teams operate in shifts, including nights or weekends.

**Build first:** networking, Windows and Linux basics, log analysis, a SIEM such as Microsoft Sentinel or Splunk, and concise incident notes. A portfolio project could show how you investigated a simulated phishing or suspicious-login event, what evidence you used, and how you reached your conclusion. Do not publish personal data or real incident details.

### 2. Incident response and digital forensics

Incident responders help organizations contain and recover from security events. Digital forensics and incident response (DFIR) work can involve preserving evidence, building timelines, identifying affected accounts and devices, and communicating findings. These roles often expect an IT or security operations foundation because incidents require technical judgment and careful evidence handling.

**Build first:** operating-system artifacts, event logs, basic scripting, evidence handling, and clear report writing. Practice only in labs or on systems you own or are authorized to examine.

### 3. Governance, risk, and compliance (GRC)

GRC professionals help an organization understand and manage security risk. They may assess controls, support audits, track remediation, review vendor risk, or help teams meet contractual and regulatory requirements. Strong GRC analysts connect a control requirement to a real business process and explain a gap in plain language.

**Build first:** risk assessment, security frameworks, policy writing, audit evidence, spreadsheets, and stakeholder communication. A useful sample project is a clearly labeled, fictional risk register for a small business.

### 4. Identity and access management (IAM)

IAM teams make sure the right people and services have appropriate access. Analysts may investigate access requests, review privileged accounts, support single sign-on, or improve joiner-mover-leaver processes. Identity skills are useful across traditional IT and cloud environments.

**Build first:** authentication and authorization, Active Directory or Entra ID fundamentals, multifactor authentication, least privilege, and audit logs. Demonstrate an access design in a lab rather than using real accounts or credentials.

### 5. Vulnerability management

Vulnerability management teams help an organization decide which weaknesses to fix first. They validate scanner results, consider whether a system is exposed or business-critical, work with asset owners, and track remediation. The value is not just producing a long scan report; it is helping teams reduce meaningful risk.

**Build first:** networking, vulnerability terminology, asset inventory, risk prioritization, and clear remediation notes. In a home lab, scan only machines you own and explain why you prioritized one finding over another.

### 6. Penetration testing and red team

Penetration testers assess security controls under an agreed scope and with written authorization. They enumerate systems, validate vulnerabilities, explain impact, and produce a report that helps the client fix issues. Red team work is usually more specialized and simulates adversary objectives across people, processes, and technology.

**Build first:** networking, Linux, web security, scripting, a repeatable testing method, and report writing. Practice in legal labs and CTFs; never test public systems without explicit authorization. Our [hands-on CTF career roadmap](/career/how-to-get-into-cybersecurity-with-ctfs/) and [cybersecurity certification guide](/certifications/best-cybersecurity-certifications-2026/) can help you build a relevant learning plan.

### 7. Application security

Application security (AppSec) professionals help product teams find and fix issues in software. The work can include threat modeling, code review, secure design guidance, testing, and improving development pipelines. Programming experience is valuable because the job involves understanding how an application is built, not only running a scanner against it.

**Build first:** one programming language, HTTP and web fundamentals, common vulnerability classes, code review basics, and constructive communication with developers. Use intentionally vulnerable applications and training labs to build examples safely.

### 8. Cloud security

Cloud security roles focus on securing identities, networks, storage, workloads, and services in platforms such as AWS, Azure, or Google Cloud. The work may involve reviewing permissions, improving logging, checking configurations, and automating guardrails. Many employers expect practical cloud or systems experience, so a certification alone is rarely enough to demonstrate readiness.

**Build first:** cloud fundamentals, IAM, network controls, encryption, logging, infrastructure as code, and one cloud provider. A portfolio project can document a small lab environment, its threat model, and the controls you configured—without exposing keys, account identifiers, or other secrets.

## Which cybersecurity job is best for beginners?

There is no universal “easiest” cybersecurity job. A role that suits a strong writer may be a poor fit for someone who wants to work in a terminal all day. For many new entrants, realistic starting points include:

- **SOC or junior security analyst**, when you can show basic networking, log analysis, and careful documentation.
- **GRC or security compliance analyst**, when your strengths include research, writing, process, and stakeholder communication.
- **IAM or vulnerability management analyst**, when you already have help-desk, systems, networking, or cloud experience.
- **IT support, help desk, or systems administration**, as a stepping stone if security employers near you ask for experience you do not yet have.

Search for “junior,” “associate,” “analyst I,” “apprentice,” “trainee,” and “security operations” alongside “cybersecurity.” Also search adjacent titles: IT support, identity administrator, vulnerability analyst, security compliance coordinator, and network technician can build relevant experience. An adjacent role is not a detour if it gives you access to systems, logs, identity workflows, or security responsibilities.

Read requirements carefully. “Entry level” is not used consistently: some employers mean a first security job, while others still expect prior IT experience. A degree may be required for some employers or public-sector roles and preferred rather than mandatory for others. The BLS describes the typical pathway for information security analysts, but also notes that some workers enter with a high school diploma and relevant training or certifications. Check the exact requirements where you plan to work.

## Skills employers look for

Start with technical foundations that connect to your target role:

- **Networking:** TCP/IP, DNS, HTTP/S, ports, routing, and how to read a basic packet capture.
- **Operating systems:** Windows accounts, event logs, permissions, services, and Linux command-line fundamentals.
- **Identity:** authentication, authorization, MFA, least privilege, and directory concepts.
- **Security concepts:** vulnerabilities, threats, risk, logging, incident response, and defense-in-depth.
- **A role-specific tool:** for example, a SIEM for SOC work, cloud IAM for cloud security, or source-code review for AppSec.
- **Scripting and data handling:** Python, PowerShell, Bash, or SQL can help automate repetitive work and investigate information.

Then demonstrate how you work. Write a short incident report, explain a detection rule, document a lab, or present a risk assessment. ISC2's early-career hiring research highlights collaboration and problem-solving as well as technical concepts. Employers want evidence that you can communicate a finding and help move it toward resolution.

## Certifications, degrees, and experience

A certification can help you learn a structured body of material or meet a requirement in a job posting. It is not a guarantee of employment, and collecting certificates without practical evidence can make a résumé harder to evaluate.

Choose based on the job descriptions you are targeting:

- For broad entry-level foundations, compare **CompTIA Security+**, **ISC2 Certified in Cybersecurity**, or a beginner course with hands-on labs.
- For a SOC path, look at SIEM and Microsoft or Splunk technologies employers in your area actually list.
- For pentesting, choose a hands-on course or certification only after building networking, Linux, and web fundamentals.
- For cloud or IAM, learn one platform and build a small, documented lab before paying for advanced credentials.

A computer science, IT, engineering, or related degree can be useful and may be required by some employers. It is one route, not the only route. Relevant IT experience, apprenticeships, military experience, labs, open-source contributions, and a focused portfolio can also help demonstrate ability. ## A practical 90-day plan to prepare for cybersecurity jobs

This plan assumes you can make steady time each week. Adjust the pace to your starting point; it is a structure for building evidence, not a promise of a job in 90 days.

### Days 1–30: choose a target and learn the basics

1. Pick one primary job family from the table above.
2. Save 15–20 real job postings in your location and highlight repeated requirements.
3. Identify the two or three skills you are missing most often.
4. Study matching foundations: networking, Windows/Linux, identity, and basic security concepts.
5. Set up a safe home lab or use a reputable training platform.

**Deliverable:** a one-page skills checklist based on actual postings and a working lab environment.

### Days 31–60: build proof you can explain

1. Complete one role-relevant project with a clear goal and scope.
2. Document your approach, evidence, result, and limitations in a short report.
3. Practice explaining the project in a three-minute walkthrough.
4. Ask someone knowledgeable to review your résumé or project notes.
5. Choose a certification only if it appears in relevant postings or fills a real knowledge gap.

**Deliverable:** one polished portfolio item and a résumé draft aligned to the target role.

### Days 61–90: apply and improve from feedback

1. Tailor your résumé to each role, using accurate language from the posting.
2. Apply to a focused set of suitable jobs each week; track the date, role, contact, and next step.
3. Use professional groups, alumni networks, meetups, or referrals to learn about teams and openings.
4. Practice role-specific interview questions and project explanations.
5. Review results every two weeks. If you get no screens, revise targeting and résumé clarity. If you get interviews but no offers, practice the skills those interviews expose.

**Deliverable:** a repeatable application routine and a short list of the next gaps to address.

## How to search and apply for cybersecurity jobs

Use a mix of job boards and employer sources. Search by job title and skill, set location and seniority filters, and check that a posting is current on the employer's own site. In the United States, federal openings are listed on [USAJOBS](https://www.usajobs.gov/); eligibility, citizenship, and clearance requirements vary by posting. The [NIST NICE Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/applied-cybersecurity/nice/nice-framework-resource-center) and [CyberSeek](https://www.cyberseek.org/) can also help explore roles and local workforce information.

For each application:

1. **Match evidence to requirements.** If a posting asks for alert triage, mention a lab investigation or relevant operations experience—only if you have actually done it.
2. **Use concrete résumé bullets.** Describe the action, tool or evidence, and result. Do not claim production experience for a home lab.
3. **Show a small, relevant portfolio.** One careful project beats a large collection of unfinished screenshots.
4. **Prepare to explain your decisions.** Interviewers may ask what you checked first, why you trusted a signal, what you would do next, and what you do not know.
5. **Ask about the work.** Clarify team structure, on-call expectations, training, tools, and how success is measured.

Be cautious with postings that promise unusually high pay for little experience, ask you to pay for equipment or training up front, or move the process to personal messaging accounts. Verify the employer and opening through its official website before sharing sensitive information.

## Frequently asked questions about cybersecurity jobs

**Can I get a cybersecurity job with no experience?**

It is possible, but competitive. Build a foundation, choose a specific role, and show evidence through a lab, project, apprenticeship, or adjacent IT experience. Entry-level postings differ, so target roles whose requirements genuinely match what you can demonstrate.

**Do cybersecurity jobs require a degree?**

Some employers require a degree, and the BLS says a bachelor's degree is typical for information security analysts. Other employers accept relevant experience, training, or certifications in place of a degree. Check local job postings and any legal or clearance rules that apply to your target role.

**What is the best cybersecurity job for beginners?**

The best starting role depends on your strengths and local openings. SOC analyst, GRC analyst, IAM analyst, vulnerability analyst, and IT support roles with security responsibilities are common routes to explore. Compare day-to-day duties, not just job titles.

**How much do cybersecurity jobs pay?**

Pay depends on role, experience, location, industry, and clearance or on-call requirements. In the United States, the BLS reported a $124,910 median annual wage for information security analysts in May 2024. That represents the middle of this occupation's pay distribution; it is not an entry-level salary or a figure for every cybersecurity role.

**Are cybersecurity jobs remote?**

Some are remote or hybrid, but many employers require on-site work, geographic eligibility, or access to restricted systems. Remote availability varies by employer, seniority, and role. Read the location and eligibility details in each posting.

**Which cybersecurity certification should I get first?**

Start with job postings for your target role. Choose a recognized entry-level certification if it appears repeatedly and helps you learn relevant fundamentals, then pair it with a hands-on project.
